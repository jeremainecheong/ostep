What an operating system does, in three pieces

A running program just executes instructions, one after another. Everything else that makes a computer usable — running many programs at once, giving each its own memory, keeping files safe — is the operating system. This lesson gives you the map for the whole book.

Primary source: Chapter 2, Introduction to Operating Systems (18 pages). Read it after this lesson; the three tiny C programs there are worth running yourself. Book code: ostep-code/intro.

What you'll be able to do

    The book's framing: crux → mechanism → policy

    OSTEP keeps asking one question, framed in a shaded box the authors call the crux. Everything in the book answers a crux with a mechanism (a low-level method, the "how") and a policy (a decision, the "which"). Keep those two words apart from the start; the book separates them on purpose so you can swap a policy without touching the mechanism.

    The crux of the whole book: how to virtualize resources. The OS takes a physical resource (CPU, memory, disk) and turns it into a more general, powerful, easy-to-use virtual version of itself. The question is never why (it makes the system easy to use) but how: what mechanisms and policies, how efficiently, and with what hardware help.

    Design goals — the trade-offs you'll see everywhere

    Abstraction is the theme under the themes. C without thinking about assembly, assembly without thinking about gates, gates without thinking about transistors. Each part of the book introduces one big abstraction: the process, the address space, the thread, the file.
